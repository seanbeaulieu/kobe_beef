How to tell real Kobe beef from fake

“Kobe” on a menu proves nothing. Most “Kobe beef” sold abroad isn't, and even in Japan plenty of places serve “wagyu” or “Kobe-style” beef and let you assume the rest. Genuine Kobe beef is traceable to a single animal, so checking takes a couple of minutes.

  1. Is the place on the official registry?

    The Kobe Beef Marketing & Distribution Promotion Association (神戸肉流通推進協議会) registers every restaurant and shop allowed to buy and sell certified Kobe beef and gives it a registration number. Registered stores display their designation certificate and a bronze statuette of a Tajima cow by the door or the counter. Every registered restaurant in Kobe is on our map and this list; for anywhere else, search the official registry.

  2. Ask for the 10-digit ID number

    Every cow in Japan carries a 10-digit individual ID (個体識別番号, kotai shikibetsu bangō) on its ear tag from birth. The association says registered butchers and restaurants display the number, so a place serving real Kobe beef can tell you the one for the animal you're eating; many write it on a board or next to the certificate. You can look it up on the National Livestock Breeding Center's cattle traceability search (Japanese, with an English version): it should show a Japanese Black (黒毛和種) heifer or steer, born and raised in Hyogo. A place that can't or won't give you a number is a bad sign.

  3. Look for the certificate and the chrysanthemum stamp

    Every carcass that passes is stamped in four places with the association's chrysanthemum mark (the nojigiku, Hyogo's prefectural flower) and gets its own Kobe Beef Certificate of Authenticity (神戸肉之証). Many restaurants frame the certificate for the animal they're currently serving. Kobe Beef is also protected as a Geographical Indication in Japan (GI No. 3, registered in 2015), so the GI mark may appear on packaging.

  4. Read the wording closely

    The protected names are 神戸ビーフ (Kobe Beef), 神戸牛 (Kobe-gyu or Kobe-ushi) and 神戸肉 (Kobe-niku). These do not mean Kobe beef:

    • “Kobe-style”, “Kobe-type”, “American Kobe”, “Kobe wagyu”: marketing, no standard behind it.
    • “Wagyu”, “A5 wagyu”, 黒毛和牛 (Japanese Black): can be excellent, but it's the breed and grade, not the brand. Kobe is a small slice of it.
    • 但馬牛 (Tajima-gyu): real and closely related (Kobe beef is Tajima beef that passed the grading), but not the same claim.
    • “Hyogo beef” or another prefecture's brand: a different product, often a good one, just not Kobe.

  5. Use price as a hint, not proof

    A big Kobe sirloin steak for a few thousand yen should make you ask for the ID. But cheap isn't automatically fake: some registered restaurants in Kobe do lunches for ¥3,000–4,000 using small portions and leaner cuts, and the typical dinner on our map is around ¥8,000–10,000. Ask which cut and how many grams.

Outside Japan

The odds are worse abroad. Only a limited amount of Kobe beef is exported each year, to a short list of certified buyers. If a restaurant abroad claims Kobe beef, it should be able to name the importer and show the certificate, and it should be on the official registry.

What makes it Kobe beef

To be sold as Kobe beef, an animal has to clear every one of these bars:

  • Tajima lineage, purebred Hyogo seed stock, bred and fattened on farms in Hyogo registered with the association.
  • Heifer or steer, not a bull or a cow that has calved, at least 28 months old.
  • Processed in Hyogo at a designated slaughterhouse.
  • Meat quality 4 or 5 and a yield grade of A or B under the Japanese grading system.
  • Marbling of BMS 6 or higher on the 12-point scale.
  • Carcass 270–499.9 kg for heifers, 300–499.9 kg for steers.

About 5,500 animals a year qualify, out of roughly 7,000 Tajima cattle brought to market. Every restaurant in Kobe that buys them is on the map.
