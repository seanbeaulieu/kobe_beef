How to tell real Kobe beef from fake

“Kobe” on a menu proves nothing. Most “Kobe beef” sold abroad isn't, and even in Japan plenty of places serve “wagyu” or “Kobe-style” beef and let you assume the rest. Genuine Kobe beef is traceable to a single animal, so checking takes a couple of minutes.

  1. Is the place on the official registry?

    The Kobe Beef Marketing & Distribution Promotion Association (神戸肉流通推進協議会) registers every restaurant and shop that buys certified Kobe beef and gives it a registration number. Registered stores usually display the certificate and a bronze statue of a Tajima cow by the door or the counter. Every registered restaurant in Kobe is on our map and this list; for anywhere else, search the official registry.

  2. Ask for the 10-digit ID number

    Every cow in Japan has a 10-digit individual ID (個体識別番号, kotai shikibetsu bangō). Places serving real Kobe beef can tell you the number for the animal you're eating, and many write it on the menu, a board or a printed certificate. You can look it up yourself on Japan's national cattle traceability service: it should show a Japanese Black (黒毛和種) heifer or steer, raised in Hyogo. A place that can't or won't give you a number is a bad sign.

  3. Look for the certificate and the Nojigiku mark

    Certified carcasses are stamped with the Nojigiku (wild chrysanthemum, Hyogo's prefectural flower) mark, and each comes with a Kobe Beef certificate carrying its ID. Many restaurants frame the certificate for the animal they're currently serving. Kobe Beef is also a registered Geographical Indication in Japan, so the GI mark may appear on packaging.

  4. Read the wording closely

    The official names are 神戸ビーフ (Kobe Beef), 神戸牛 (Kobe-gyu) and 神戸肉 (Kobe-niku). These do not mean Kobe beef:

    • “Kobe-style”, “Kobe-type”, “American Kobe”, “Kobe wagyu”: marketing, no standard behind it.
    • “Wagyu”, “A5 wagyu”, 黒毛和牛 (Japanese Black): can be excellent, but it's the breed and grade, not the brand. Kobe is a small slice of it.
    • 但馬牛 (Tajima-gyu): real and closely related (Kobe beef is Tajima beef that passed the grading), but not the same claim.
    • “Hyogo beef” or another prefecture's brand: a different product, often a good one, just not Kobe.

  5. Use price as a hint, not proof

    A big Kobe sirloin steak for a few thousand yen should make you ask for the ID. But cheap isn't automatically fake: registered steak houses in Kobe serve Kobe beef lunches from about ¥2,000–4,000, using small portions and leaner cuts, and the typical dinner course on our map is around ¥10,000. Ask which cut and how many grams.

Outside Japan the odds are worse. Only a limited amount of Kobe beef is exported each year, to a short list of certified buyers. If a restaurant abroad claims Kobe beef, it should be able to name the importer and show the certificate, and it should be on the official registry.

What makes it Kobe beef

To be sold as Kobe beef, an animal has to clear every one of these bars:

  • Tajima lineage, born and raised on registered farms in Hyogo Prefecture.
  • Heifer or steer, not a bull or breeding cow.
  • Processed in Hyogo at a designated slaughterhouse.
  • Graded 4 or 5 for meat quality, with a yield grade of A or B.
  • Marbling of BMS 6 or higher on the 12-point scale.
  • Carcass weight under 500 kg.

That's why it's scarce: only a few thousand animals a year qualify. Ready to eat some? Find a registered restaurant on the map.
